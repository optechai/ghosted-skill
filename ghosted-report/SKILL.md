## How to answer

1. **Answer in the language the person writes in.** Keep the report's terms recognizable (the retention window, acting in the window, signals, contacted before deciding, still weighing it up).
2. **Numbers first, short.** Lead with the figure that answers the question, then 2 to 4 supporting points. No preamble.
3. **Only use numbers from this file.** The report covers US adults only, one survey wave, unweighted. The only breakdowns are by product category (fintech, insurance, health), and only for the figures listed under "Category splits" below. There are **no breakdowns by age or generation, income, gender, region, country, company, brand or company size**. If someone asks for a cut the report doesn't have ("Gen Z vs boomers", "UK customers", "banks vs credit cards"), say plainly that the report doesn't break it down and give the overall figure. Never invent a breakdown and present it as the report's.
4. **Keep the report's wording. These are the guardrails:**
   - **The survey does not measure why people left or what caused it.** 87% weren't contacted before they decided; 64% never heard from their provider at all. Never say "87% left because nobody contacted them" or "lack of contact causes churn". Say "87% of customers who left heard nothing from their provider before they decided to go."
   - **Getting in touch does not, on its own, make customers stay.** Among the 702 who had seriously thought about leaving, 41% of those contacted first stayed, against 26% of those who weren't. But that gap **shrinks to almost nothing** when people who had got equally far in their decision are compared: people who were contacted were more often still early (only thinking about it or looking around). The survey can't tell whether contact kept them early or providers reached people who happened to be early. Never claim contact causes retention, and never quote "41% vs 26%" without this caveat.
   - **76% vs 40% (fix vs sell) is directional, with small bases.** It is among people who were **still only thinking about leaving or looking around** when they made their final choice, not all customers. Bases: fixed a problem 76% (59), discount 55% (100), checked in 53% (36), cheaper plan 51% (86), explained a change 51% (57), tried to sell something else 40% (45), didn't get in touch first 61% (136). Always give the bases and say "directional". Fixing a problem was the **only** kind of contact with a higher stay rate than no contact at all (76% vs 61%).
   - **The 61% no-contact stay rate and the 26% no-contact stay rate are different groups.** 61% (base 136) is people still early in their decision; 26% is everyone among the 702 who wasn't contacted first. Don't mix them.
   - **$826 and $8.3m are estimates.** Always "we estimate" / "about", "an illustration of the maths, not a forecast". How it was built: for each respondent, what they paid a year x how much longer they say they'd have stayed x the chance the fix would have worked x whether the provider could have caught them in time (took a week or longer to leave, left a signal, and wasn't contacted before deciding). High end of each band; $500 or more a month counts as $500, more than 5 years counts as 5 years. $826 is the average across the **765 respondents who paid something and gave an amount**; it is **$658** if customers who paid nothing directly count as zero. **Most respondents contribute nothing**; a small group of high-spending customers who say they'd have stayed for years makes up most of the total. Bank accounts and investing apps undercount, because customers there often pay no fees directly. A provider's own revenue per customer gives a better figure. $8.3m is simply $826 x 10,000 customers lost a year.
   - **"What would have kept you" answers are stated intent**, from people recalling a decision. 84% named one thing; 65% of those say they would definitely or probably have stayed. Never present these as proven retention rates.
   - **58% vs 79%: keep both, don't merge.** 58% say their provider had no way of knowing they were unhappy, yet 79% did something their provider could see. Quote them side by side as the report does; don't net them or say "58% were wrong".
   - **Same number, different stat.** Two different 84%s (named one thing that would have kept them; say companies only pay attention when they try to leave). Two different 87%s (weren't contacted before deciding; say their old provider treated them like every other customer). Two different 72%s (took a week or longer; of those who named a fix, picked something within the provider's reach). Two different 40%s (took more than a month; stay rate when the provider tried to sell something else). Name which one you mean.
   - **Two "explain again" figures.** 15% of all respondents had to explain their situation again each time. 30% of customers **who got in touch about a problem** had to. Same for repeat contacts: 18% of all respondents vs 56% of those who got in touch about a problem. The report doesn't publish how many got in touch about a problem; don't back it out.
   - **Recall bias and panel.** People recalling a decision from up to a year ago tend to blame the company; online panelists lean tech-savvy; results are unweighted. Margin of error about ±3.1 points for the full sample, wider for subgroups.
   - **Finding numbers differ between the report and the blog.** The blog leads with the $826 finding. Refer to findings by name, not number, unless the person cites a number. The five priorities (What leaders should do now) are numbered the same everywhere, so "priority 02 (watch the signals you already have)" is fine.
   - **Where the 702 come from.** Every one of the 1,000 respondents was also asked whether they had seriously thought about leaving a company in these categories, and what they did. 702 of them had either left or stayed, which is the base for the 41% vs 26% comparison. The stay-rate table (76%, 40% and so on) is a subset of those 702: people still only thinking about it or looking around when they made their final choice.
   - **"Too late for most"** (about contact after the decision) is the report's own wording, not a measured stay rate. The report publishes no stay rate for contact after deciding. Quote it as the report's view.
   - **Win-back after cancellation is not measured.** The stay rates are for contact while people were still deciding. Don't apply 76%, 55% or 40% to a win-back or exit offer sent after someone has cancelled; say the report doesn't measure win-back.
5. **Cite the source** once per answer: "Lorikeet, Ghosted (November 2026)". Add the report link above when the person wants to read more.
6. **Keep the kinds of evidence apart.** Percentages come from the survey. Quotes come from an open-ended question and illustrate, they don't measure. $826 and $8.3m are Lorikeet's modelled estimates. Derived figures (see table) are arithmetic on published numbers, labelled as such.
7. **Arithmetic is allowed, labelled.** If someone gives their own customer numbers, you may apply the report's rates, but say it is an illustration using US survey rates, not a forecast and not a number from the report.

### Retention window scorecard (a company describes its setup)

The person describes how they spot and handle customers who might leave and asks whether they're acting in time or what to fix. Work from what they tell you. If something isn't covered, mark it **Can't tell** instead of guessing.

**Step 1. Read their setup.** Note each item:

- Signal watching: do they watch the signals customers already give (usage drops, questions about fees or cancelling, money moving out or autopay stopped, the same problem raised twice)? Are support contacts linked to account health?
- Timing of outreach: do they get in touch before the customer decides, or only once a cancellation arrives (win-back, save desk, exit offers)?
- What outreach leads with: fixing the problem the customer has, or a discount, a cheaper plan, a check-in, or a cross-sell or upsell?
- Memory: does whoever gets in touch (or whoever the customer reaches) know what the customer already said, so they don't have to explain it again?
- Measurement: do they measure what happens after contact, by the kind of contact and by how far along the customer was?

**Step 2. Scorecard vs. the report.** One table: Area | Yours | What the report found | Rating. Ratings:

- **Ready.** Matches what the report says acting in the window means.
- **Gap.** Missing or weaker than what the report points to, and the report gives a reason it matters (cite the number). Example: outreach that leads with a discount rather than a fix (55%, base 100, vs 76% for a fix, base 59; directional).
- **Risk.** Something that works against the report's findings: outreach that only starts after the cancellation (87% weren't contacted before deciding; 23% heard only after they'd decided, too late for most), outreach that leads with selling something else (40% stayed, the lowest, base 45, directional), or making customers repeat themselves (30% of those who got in touch about a problem had to).
- **Can't tell.** What they shared doesn't cover it.

If support tickets aren't linked to account health, rate both Signal watching and Memory as Gap: whoever reaches out can't see what the customer already raised. If their only outreach is a win-back or exit offer after cancelling, rate Timing as Risk and say the report's stay rates don't measure post-cancellation offers; rate What outreach leads with on what that message leads with, without applying the stay rates to it.

## Methodology

- **Sample:** 1,000 US adults who chose to leave or not renew a fintech, insurance or health product in the last 12 months.
- **Panel:** a national online research panel.
- **Products:** fintech (592: bank accounts, credit cards, buy now pay later, loans, investing and payments apps), insurance (291: health, car, home, life, pet, travel), health (116: telehealth, therapy apps, pharmacy subscriptions). Respondents answered about the product they left most recently. (The published category bases sum to 999.)
- **Survey:** 16 questions, run by Lorikeet, with no brand and no mention of AI. Health questions asked about the service, never about conditions or treatment.
- **Data collected:** October 28, 2026.
- **Margin of error:** about ±3.1 points for the full sample, wider for subgroups (bases shown).
- **Stayers:** everyone was also asked whether they had seriously thought about leaving a company in these categories, and what they did. 702 had either left or stayed, which allows a comparison of the two.
- **Note:** people recalling a decision from up to a year ago tend to blame the company, and online panelists lean tech-savvy. Results are unweighted. "What would have kept you" answers are stated intent.

## Idea in brief

- **The problem.** Customers of banks, insurers and health providers take weeks to leave, and they leave signals along the way. Most providers find out when the cancellation arrives.
- **The evidence.** In a survey of 1,000 US adults who left a fintech, insurance or health provider, 72% took a week or longer to go. 79% did something their provider could see. 87% weren't contacted before they decided.
- **The stakes.** We estimate the average customer who left took about $826 in revenue with them that could have been kept. For a provider that loses 10,000 customers a year, that would be about $8.3m.
- **The response.** Watch for the signals customers already give. Get in touch before they decide, lead with fixing the problem, and remember what they've already told you.

Report subtitle: a survey of 1,000 US consumers who left a bank, insurer or health provider finds that most gave weeks of warning, and that 87% heard nothing from their provider before they decided to go.

## Key findings

- **72%** took a week or longer to leave. 40% took more than a month.
- **79%** did something their provider could see before they left, like using it less, asking how to cancel or moving money out.
- **87%** weren't contacted before they decided to leave. 64% never heard from their provider at all.
- **76% vs 40%.** Among customers still weighing it up, 76% stayed when their provider got in touch and fixed a problem. 40% stayed when it tried to sell them something else. (Directional, bases 59 and 45.)
- **84%** named one thing that would have kept them. The top answer was "fixing my problem the first time" (19%).
- **84%** say companies only pay attention to them when they try to leave.
- **About $826** in revenue that could have been kept, per customer who left (our estimate).

## Finding: Customers take weeks to leave

**72% took a week or longer between first thinking about leaving and leaving. 40% took more than a month.**

Question: how long was it between first thinking about leaving and leaving? Base: all respondents (n = 1,000).

| Time to leave | % |
|---|---|
| Same day | 7 |
| 1 to 6 days | 19 |
| 1 to 4 weeks | 31 |
| 1 to 3 months | 28 |
| More than 3 months | 12 |
| Not sure | 2 |

Few customers leave on impulse. 7% left the same day they first thought about it. Most spent weeks deciding: 31% took one to four weeks, 28% one to three months and 12% longer than that.

## Finding: They show it

**79% did something their provider could see before they left.**

| Signal before leaving | % |
|---|---|
| Used it less | 32 |
| Asked about fees or how to cancel | 24 |
| Moved money out or stopped autopay | 22 |
| Contacted them more than once about the same problem | 18 |
| Had to explain their situation again each time | 15 |
| Contacted them once about a problem | 15 |
| Made a formal complaint | 11 |
| Missed a payment or appointment | 9 |
| Downgraded their plan or cover | 7 |
| Gave a low rating or survey score | 7 |

The most common signals were quiet ones: using the product less, asking about fees or how to cancel, and moving money out or stopping autopay. 59% did both: they took a week or longer, and they left a signal along the way.

Customers who got in touch about a problem often had to keep trying. 56% of them contacted their provider more than once about the same problem. 30% had to explain their situation again each time.

## Finding: Almost none heard from their provider in time

**87% weren't contacted before they decided to leave. 64% never heard from their provider at all.**

Question: before you cancelled, did the provider get in touch? Base: all respondents (n = 1,000).

| Did the provider get in touch? | % |
|---|---|
| Yes, before they'd decided to leave | 12 |
| Yes, but only after they'd decided | 23 |
| No | 64 |
| Not sure | 1 |

Only 12% heard from their provider before they made up their mind. Another 23% heard only after they had decided. 87% = 23% + 64%.

**Category splits (not contacted before deciding):** fintech 85% (base 592), insurance 92% (base 291), health 80% (base 116).

## Finding: When providers do get in touch, what they say matters

**Among customers still weighing it up, 76% stayed when their provider fixed a problem. 40% stayed when it tried to sell them something else.**

Stay rate by what the provider did when it got in touch first. Base: people who seriously thought about leaving and were still only thinking about it or looking around when they made their final choice. Bases in brackets.

| What the provider did | Stayed | Base |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed a problem they had | 76% | 59 |
| Offered a discount or better price | 55% | 100 |
| Just checked in | 53% | 36 |
| Suggested a cheaper or better plan | 51% | 86 |
| Explained a change, like a price increase | 51% | 57 |
| Tried to sell them something else | 40% | 45 |
| Didn't get in touch first (reference line) | 61% | 136 |

- Among the 702 people who had seriously thought about leaving, 41% of those contacted first stayed, against 26% of those who weren't. That gap shrinks to almost nothing when people who had got equally far in their decision are compared. People who were contacted first were more often still early, only thinking about it or looking around. |---|---|

| Telling me about a cheaper plan or better fit they already had | 16 |

**84% say companies only pay attention to them when they try to leave. 87% say their old provider treated them like every other customer.**

Paired statements (respondents chose which is closer to their view). Base: all respondents (n = 1,000).

| Statement A | % | Statement B | % |
|---|---|---|---|

- 54% agreed strongly that companies only pay attention when they try to leave. 61% agreed strongly that their old provider treated them like every other customer.

- For a provider that loses 10,000 customers a year, $826 each would be about $8.3m. This is an illustration of the maths, not a forecast. A provider's own revenue per customer gives a better figure.
- Bank accounts and investing apps undercount, because customers there often pay no fees directly.

## What acting in the window means

A provider that acts in the window notices the signals customers already give: lower usage, questions about fees or cancelling, money moving out, the same problem raised twice. It gets in touch before the customer decides. It leads with fixing the problem. It remembers what the customer has already said, so they don't have to explain it again.

## What leaders should do now

Five priorities follow from the data.

1. **Treat the weeks before a cancellation as the retention window.** 72% of customers took a week or longer to leave. That is time to act.
2. **Watch the signals you already have.** 79% did something their provider could see. Usage drops, cancellation questions, money moving out and repeat contacts are all in your systems today.
3. **Get in touch before they decide.** 87% heard nothing before they made up their mind. A message that arrives after the decision is too late for most.
4. **Lead with the fix.** Fixing a problem had the highest stay rate of any kind of contact. Trying to sell something else had the lowest.
5. **Stop making customers repeat themselves.** 30% of customers who got in touch about a problem had to explain their situation again each time.
